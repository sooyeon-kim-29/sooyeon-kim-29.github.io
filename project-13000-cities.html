Air quality, associated health burdens, and CO₂ emissions across 13,000 global cities

More than half of the world's population lives in cities, where air pollution and greenhouse gas emissions are concentrated. The 13,000 Cities Project uses large geospatial datasets to track air quality, emissions, and health across more than 13,000 urban areas worldwide. My work in this project has two parts:

Published · 2025 Study 1. Two decades of urban air pollution and CO₂ trends In preparation Study 2. Development, air pollution, and disease burden
Published · 2025

Study 1. Tracking air pollution and CO₂ emissions in 13,189 urban areas worldwide

The question

How have urban air pollution and CO₂ emissions changed worldwide over two decades, and do pollutants and emissions move together?

Approach

I led the development of a harmonized global database of PM₂.₅, NO₂, ozone, and fossil-fuel CO₂ emissions for 13,189 urban areas, built from large geospatial datasets, and analyzed long-term trends from 2000 to 2020.

Key findings

  • This was the first study summarizing city-level pollution and emissions trends worldwide from 2000 to 2020.
  • Ozone increased in cities globally over the study period.
  • Temporal trends of air quality and CO₂ emissions varied much depending on the global region, reflecting different policies and major emission sources of those.
  • We observed correlations among air pollutants and CO₂ emissions during the study period, which points to opportunities for coordinated air quality and climate policies.

In preparation

Study 2. City-level development, air pollution, and pollution-related disease burdens

The question

As cities develop, how does air pollution change, revisiting an environmental Kuznets curve? And how does a city's level of human development shape the disease burden its residents face from air pollution?

Approach

I model the associations between the Human Development Index (HDI) and PM₂.₅, NO₂, and ozone across ~13,000 cities using Bayesian hierarchical spatial regression (R-INLA), accounting for spatial structure and country-level differences. I then estimate pollution-attributable mortality with Global Burden of Disease methods and compare current burdens with a scenario in which cities meet WHO air quality guidelines.

Results In preparation

Results are in preparation for publication.
